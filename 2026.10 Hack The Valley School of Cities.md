# Hack the Valley x School of Cities

Challenge: Build a civic technology product that helps people understand, navigate, or respond to a real urban problem facing Toronto in the 2026 municipal election.

* Teams should identify a concrete problem faced by residents, journalists, community organizations, policymakers, or other civic users, and build a useful prototype in response.  
* Projects should use Toronto urban data as a foundation, but remain open in approach. The goal is not to predict the election or support a candidate, but to make civic information more useful, accessible, and actionable.

A strong submission should answer:

* Who is the user?  
* What problem(s) are they facing?  
* How can we solve that problem using the data we have available?  
* How can this solution benefit the user?  
* Include links to all data sources used

# Priority Problem Areas

Here are a few examples to help guide your thinking. These examples are not intended to be limiting – get creative and think up additional problems that you think need solving\!

## Housing and Affordability

Housing costs and development pressures vary widely across Toronto, but useful information is often fragmented. Possible directions:

* **Where can I actually afford to live?** Compare rent, income, transit access, commute costs, and neighbourhood characteristics.  
* **Finding a rental shouldn't require twenty Facebook groups.** Build a better renter information, roommate matching, or neighbourhood comparison tool.  
* **Which neighbourhoods are under the most housing pressure?** Explore affordability, renter vulnerability, redevelopment, or loss of lower-cost rental stock.  
* **Where is the cheapest place to buy groceries near me?** Combine grocery locations, prices or flyers, transit access, and neighbourhood income to help users find lower-cost options.  
* **What does “affordable” really mean once transportation is included?** Compare housing costs with commute time, transit fares, and car dependency.

## Transit, Congestion, and Access

Travel times, reliability, congestion, and street space are everyday problems across Toronto. Possible directions:

* **Bikes vs. cars vs. pedestrians: can Toronto streets work for everyone?** Explore collisions, bike lanes, sidewalks, traffic volumes, and road space.  
* **Why is my streetcar so slow?** Use delays, routes, traffic, stop spacing, or street design to explain where service breaks down.  
* **How long does Toronto really take to cross?** Build a travel-time or accessibility tool for jobs, schools, hospitals, recreation, or other destinations.  
* **What would changing how we use road space actually do?** Build a congestion, transit-priority, cycling, or street-design scenario explorer.  
* **Where are people most dependent on cars because the alternatives are weak?** Compare transit access, commute mode, travel time, and neighbourhood demographics.  
* **How unreliable is my trip?** Build a tool that shows not just average travel time, but the likelihood of delays or missed connections.

## Youth, Safety, and Opportunity

Young people have unequal access to employment, recreation, transit, public space, and community facilities. Possible directions:

* **What can a teenager actually do near home?** Build a tool for finding recreation, libraries, community programs, sports facilities, or public spaces.  
* **Where are the opportunities hardest to reach without a car?** Compare youth population with transit access, jobs, recreation, and services.  
* **Where could a youth corps have the biggest impact?** Match neighbourhood needs with projects involving parks, public space, climate resilience, community services, or local infrastructure.  
* **Can young people find programs that actually fit them?** Build a recommendation or matching tool based on age, location, interests, hours, cost, and transit access.  
* **Where are safe routes to school, recreation, or transit weakest?** Combine collisions, street design, crossings, cycling infrastructure, and pedestrian networks.

## Other 

Teams can also tackle other timely Toronto problems, such as:

* **Can you find a public washroom when you actually need one?** Build a locator that accounts for hours, accessibility, proximity, and transit.  
* **Could 311 data make City service requests easier to track or understand?** Show recurring problems, response patterns, or what issues are most common nearby.  
* **Where is Toronto growing faster than its infrastructure?** Compare population and development with transit, parks, schools, recreation, or other services.  
* **Can residents understand what is being built around them before construction starts?** Turn development applications and permits into a simple neighbourhood change tool.  
* **Where are City services hardest to reach?** Identify areas where important services exist but are difficult to access without a car.  
* **Can an AI tool answer “What is happening in my neighbourhood?”** Combine multiple civic datasets into a grounded, location-specific explanation rather than a generic chatbot.

# Product Expectations

Projects should result in a **usable prototype**, not only an analysis. Possible formats include:

* AI-powered civic assistant  
* interactive map  
* ward or neighbourhood tool  
* issue or resource navigator  
* recommendation or matching system  
* scenario explorer  
* report-card or briefing generator  
* prototype app for residents, journalists, community organizations, or policymakers

Teams are encouraged to use responsive AI where it adds real value, rather than just a chatbot layer, such as:

* answering questions about neighbourhood data  
* generating plain-language explanations  
* producing customized ward summaries  
* helping users compare places or scenarios  
* recommending relevant resources

## Data

The School of Cities can provide a Toronto Civic Data Starter Guide, pointing to resources including:

* ward, neighbourhood, and census tract geographies  
* census demographics and socioeconomic indicators  
* election results and turnout  
* municipal campaign finance data  
* transit, commute, and accessibility indicators  
* libraries, parks, community centres, shelters, recreation facilities, and other civic infrastructure  
* development applications and building permits

## Judging

Projects could be scored on:

* Usefulness: Does it solve a clear civic problem?  
* Creativity: Is the approach original?  
* Urban insight: Does it make meaningful use of Toronto data?  
* Technical execution: Does the prototype work?  
* AI integration: Is AI used with intention/meaningfully?  
* Accessibility: Could the intended audience realistically use it?  
* Responsible interpretation: Are limitations and sensitive neighbourhood comparisons handled appropriately?
